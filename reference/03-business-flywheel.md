# THE BUSINESS FLYWHEEL: MAKING INPUTS AND OUTPUTS VISIBLE

*Background reading for the **Stuck** phase of The Breakout Stack. Referenced by the skills that use these concepts -- not required reading up front.*

### Why the Flywheel Matters

The founder is inside the machine. They see the pieces they touch, the decisions they make, the results that show up. But they don't see the whole machine. They can't. They're too close.

The Business Flywheel is a simple one-page framework that makes the whole machine visible. Not perfectly — it's a simplification. But visibly. Enough to see which parts are working, which are broken, and which is the bottleneck.

A flywheel has three components: inputs, segments, and outputs (handoffs). For effectively every software company, the structure is:

**Core inputs:** The money and effort that feed each segment.  
**Segments:** Innovation, Demand Generation, Sales Conversion, Customer Success, Cash Flow Assurance, Capacity Generation — the six segments that move revenue.  
**Core outputs:** ARR, customer acquisition, retention rate, unit economics, runway, organizational capacity — the results that signal whether each segment is working.

### The Six Segments

**Segment 1: Innovation** — What are you building? Is it solving the problem customers will pay for? The input is engineering effort. The output, handed to Demand Generation, is a marketable feature customers actually want.

Kevin at Icetana had a strong product (security AI that actually works). But the output wasn't just "product exists." The output was "product wins in competitive deals against established vendors." That's a different measurement than "we shipped the feature." It's "do customers choose us?"

**Segment 2: Demand Generation** — How do you turn marketable features into an excited, hand-raising audience? The input is ICP definition, messaging, channels, an owner. The output, handed to Sales Conversion, is leads or signups.

**Segment 3: Sales Conversion** — How do customers find you and get convinced to pay? The input is sales effort and capital spent. The output, handed to Customer Success, is closed deals. Kevin's sales had an input (him, personally closing every deal) but no owner, and no repeatable machine. The output was "Kevin gets deals done sometimes." That's not a system. A system would be "our enterprise sales playbook closes 30% of qualified pipeline." (This is also where founders most often misdiagnose: "sales is broken" is very often "demand generation is broken" wearing a sales costume — a sales team can't convert an audience that was never generated in the first place.)

**Segment 4: Customer Success** — How do you deliver value once the customer pays, and keep them renewing and expanding? The input is delivery resources (support, implementation, training). The output, fed back into the loop, is renewals, expansion, net revenue retention. A weak Customer Success segment looks like "we shipped it, it's the customer's problem now." A strong one looks like "we ensure every customer hits their success metrics." Companies with strong retention can 3x growth with the same sales spend (the existing customer base grows faster).

**Segment 5: Cash Flow Assurance** — Is the business collecting on time, controlling spend, and converting growth into investor interest that fronts cash before the business self-funds? The output, handed to Capacity Generation, is runway.

**Segment 6: Capacity Generation** — Does the organization have enough human and operational capacity to perform — hiring, but also legal, IT, knowledge management, ops, even executive management? The output feeds back into Innovation, closing the loop: more capacity → more marketable features → more demand → more conversion → more revenue → more runway → reinvest in capacity.

### How the Flywheel Works (or Breaks)

A healthy flywheel has each segment feeding the next:

- Innovation ships marketable features → Demand Generation turns them into an audience → Sales Conversion turns that audience into customers → Customer Success keeps them renewing and expanding → Cash Flow Assurance turns growth into runway → Capacity Generation reinvests that runway into more capacity → more capacity produces more marketable features → the cycle repeats

When one segment breaks, it breaks the whole flywheel:

- If Innovation is broken, Demand Generation has nothing credible to promote (or promotes something and gets angry customers)
- If Demand Generation is broken, even a great product never reaches enough of an audience for Sales Conversion to work with
- If Sales Conversion is broken, a real audience never becomes revenue
- If Customer Success is broken, customers leave fast — high churn, weak retention
- If Cash Flow Assurance is broken, growth on paper doesn't translate into runway to fund the next quarter
- If Capacity Generation is broken, every other segment is starved of the people and systems needed to run

Kevin's flywheel had a specific break point: Sales Conversion was broken (no owner, no system, only founder). This meant:
- Pipeline was dependent on Kevin's network and time
- Close rates were unpredictable (varied by deal)
- New customers weren't expanding because Kevin was too busy with new deals to nurture them
- Innovation was improving but couldn't demonstrate value in market because sales wasn't systematic enough to show the difference
- Customer Success was suffering because Kevin wasn't setting up success properly once the customer signed

Fix Sales Conversion, and the whole flywheel accelerates.

### How Founders Get Stuck

Founders typically see their own function clearly. They know whether the product is working or not. They can feel when sales is broken.

What they can't see: which broken segment is the actual constraint right now.

A founder might diagnose: "Our product isn't competitive enough. We need to rebuild." Three years of engineering work later, they realize: the product was actually fine. The problem was Sales Conversion. Nobody knew about it.

Another founder diagnoses: "Our retention is terrible. We need better success resources." They hire customer success VPs. The output is the same. The constraint wasn't Customer Success. It was that customers were never the right fit to begin with (Sales Conversion brought in bad deals — or Demand Generation never targeted the right audience).

The flywheel makes this visible. It forces the diagnosis to be systemic, not intuitive.

Kevin's flywheel work surfaced this clearly: Sales Conversion was the constraint. Not because it was broken per se, but because the team's ability to scale every other segment was contingent on it being fixed first. You could improve Innovation and it wouldn't matter (still couldn't sell it). You could improve Customer Success and it wouldn't matter (still only serving customers Kevin personally closed).

### Using the Flywheel Monthly and Quarterly

**Monthly:** Is each segment running independently? Did Innovation ship the things planned? Is Demand Generation hitting lead targets? Is Sales Conversion hitting pipeline-to-close targets? Is Customer Success holding retention steady? Is Cash Flow Assurance keeping runway healthy? Is Capacity Generation keeping the org staffed and productive?

If any segment is stalling, you now know which one to focus on. You don't guess. You see it.

**Quarterly:** Did the flywheel's output change? ARR up? Customer acquisition down? Retention shifting? Runway shrinking? Which segment's shift produced that output? Force-rank all six red / yellow / green — which two are reddest, and within those two, which is the absolute darkest red?

This makes the quarterly breakthrough decision clear: which segment's constraint, if removed, would accelerate the whole flywheel?
