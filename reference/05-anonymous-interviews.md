# ANONYMOUS LEADERSHIP INTERVIEWS: SURFACING WHAT PEOPLE WON'T SAY

*Background reading for the **Stuck** phase of The Breakout Stack. Referenced by the skills that use these concepts -- not required reading up front.*

### The Problem They Solve

A founder asks the leadership team: "What's the real bottleneck in our company?" In a meeting, the team says: "We need clearer processes. We need better tools. We need to align on roadmap." Safe answers. Deferential answers. Answers that don't challenge the founder.

The same founder asks the same question in a confidential, anonymous interview: "From the CEO perspective, what's genuinely broken in this company?" The answer changes. The real issue surfaces: "The CEO is in every deal and we can't move without them." Or: "The sales leader is toxic and everyone on the team knows it but we can't say it because they're the founder's friend." Or: "We're spread too thin across four markets and we should kill three of them, but the CEO won't choose."

The difference: attribution. When the team member's name is attached to the feedback, they filter through fear. When it's confidential, they tell the truth.

### How It Works

**Step 1: Design the Interview.** The same three questions to every leader:

1. From the CEO's perspective (not your functional perspective), what's genuinely strong in this company right now?
2. From the CEO's perspective, what's genuinely broken or weak right now?
3. Where's the real bottleneck — the one constraint that, if solved, would unlock everything else?

The framing ("from the CEO's perspective") shifts the conversation away from "my department is fine, but marketing sucks." It forces systems thinking.

**Step 2: Conduct the Interviews.** Individually. Private. Not the founder. (An outside facilitator is ideal, but a trusted person in the organization works if confidentiality is absolute.) Record the answer or take notes. The leader talks. The interviewer listens.

The key: no follow-up opinions. No "don't worry, the CEO is working on that." Just listen. The goal is to hear what the leader actually thinks, not to reassure them.

**Step 3: Compile Without Attribution.** Read through all the interviews. Mark common themes. Identify contradictions. Note what's brought up by multiple people (signal that it's not one person's perception, it's a pattern). Organize by topic, not by person.

Create a summary document: "What we're hearing from the leadership team." No names. No "Kevin said this and Raf said that." Just: "3 of 6 leaders mentioned that sales ownership is unclear." "All 6 leaders cited runway pressure as a major constraint." "4 of 6 leaders said the founder is in too many decisions."

**Step 4: Share With the Founder (and the Team).** The founder reads the anonymous summary and sees their blind spot in black and white. Usually this is shocking. The founder thought the team was aligned. They read: the team is frustrated. The founder thought sales was the problem. They read: lack of clear roles is the problem. The founder thought the company's sense of purpose was clear. They read: people are doing their jobs but don't know what they're fighting for.

Share the same summary with the leadership team (without attribution). The team's reaction: relief. "I thought I was the only one thinking this." The team realizes it's not just one person's complaint. It's a pattern. This gives permission for real conversation.

**Step 5: Diagnose Together.** In the next leadership workshop, use the anonymous feedback as the data. "The team's feedback says X is the constraint. Is that accurate? What would it take to address it? What's the real breakthrough?" Now the conversation isn't "founder's intuition vs. team's opinion." It's "here's what's true according to our own team's assessment — how do we address it?"

### Common Findings (and What They Mean)

**Pattern 1: "The founder is the bottleneck."**

What it means: Too many decisions route through the founder. Teams are waiting. Speed is compromised. Authority is unclear.

What to do: Announce a clear change in authority. Specific decisions the team owns (with no founder override). Run the structured workshops where the founder speaks last. Create windows of wisdom where the team decides without you.

Kevin's interviews would have surfaced this explicitly: the team doesn't know who owns sales because Kevin owns it (but hasn't admitted it). The feedback would've been: "We need a clearer owner." The diagnosis: we need to change the structure so someone can own it. That's what Icetana did — brought on Ryan to own major account execution, explicitly reducing Kevin's day-to-day involvement.

**Pattern 2: "We don't know what we're fighting for."**

What it means: The company's narrative is unclear. The team is executing tasks but not connected to purpose. Work feels like chores, not mission.

What to do: This is a clarity problem + communication problem. The founder has a narrative in their head (often implicit). The team doesn't. The quarterly breakthrough workshop is where this gets built together. The breakthrough becomes the narrative: "This quarter, we're solving X. This is why it matters. This is what changes when we nail it."

Kevin's team said: "We do our jobs but couldn't tell you what we're fighting for" (1/5 on purpose). The solution wasn't a better mission statement. It was a breakthrough workshop where the team built a shared narrative about what the company was actually fighting for (a repeatable commercial engine) and what the quarterly breakthrough was (building sales systemization).

**Pattern 3: "Leadership team doesn't trust each other."**

What it means: Silos. Teams not coordinating. Each function protecting their own interests instead of building together. Dysfunction.

What to do: The anonymous feedback surfaces the mistrust. Acknowledge it. Bring it to the workshop: "Our own team said trust is an issue. What's actually happening?" Usually, it's structural: unclear roles, unclear authority, unclear decision-making. Fix the structure and trust rebuilds.

**Pattern 4: "We don't have time to think strategically."**

What it means: The team is in execution mode constantly. No space for planning, diagnosis, or breakthrough thinking. Days are full of meetings and responsive work.

What to do: Block time. The quarterly workshop is protected time. The weekly rocks check-in is structured time (15 min, not 2 hours). Protect strategic time for each function (one day per week for the sales leader to think about sales strategy, not just execute). The founder's job is to defend this time from getting eaten by crises.

### Why Anonymous Feedback Shifts Everything

The anonymous format changes what's possible:

- A team member can name the founder as the bottleneck (which they'd never say in person)
- A leader can admit they don't know how to do their job (which feels like weakness in a meeting)
- The team can surface grievances and hope for change (which would feel like complaint otherwise)
- The founder can see themselves as others see them (which is always uncomfortable but clarifying)

The data becomes your truth-teller. Not "I think the founder is the problem." But "the team assessment says the founder is the constraint." Harder to argue with. More likely to drive change.

Kevin's feedback would have named sales ownership as the missing piece. Rik's feedback at InRule would have surfaced the hub-and-spoke model. Luke's feedback at Redox would have identified the need for delegation and distributed authority. All of it was visible once attribution disappeared.
