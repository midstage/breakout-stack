# WINDOWS OF WISDOM: HOW TO LET YOUR TEAM THINK

*Background reading for the **Stick** phase of The Breakout Stack. Referenced by the skills that use these concepts -- not required reading up front.*

### The Core Insight

A team learns to think when the founder isn't in the room answering. The founder's presence, no matter how well-intentioned, signals: "I'll figure this out." The team's response is: "Okay, we'll wait."

Windows of Wisdom are structured conditions where the team has to think independently. The founder is absent or silent on purpose. The team faces a problem, they reason through it, they decide, they execute. Gradually, the team's thinking muscle rebuilds.

This is not delegation that the founder overrides. This is not "I'll give them authority and then take it back when I disagree." This is real authority where the team knows: the founder is not in the loop on purpose. We have to get this right.

### Why the Founder's Presence Kills Thinking

The dynamic is neurological, not character-based. When a smart authority figure is in the room, the team's prefrontal cortex (reasoning, creativity, independent thought) quiets down. Instead, the team activates a threat-response (how do I look smart? how do I avoid being wrong?). This is not conscious. It's how humans are wired around authority.

The team isn't being lazy or stupid. They're being adaptive. They're responding to the structure: "The founder is here, the founder has the answer, I should listen."

Remove the founder from the room, and the team's thinking activates. They have to reason through the problem. They propose ideas. They disagree with each other. They reach consensus. The work is harder in the moment, but the learning is real.

### Three Structures That Create Windows of Wisdom

**Structure 1: The Quarterly Leadership Workshop**

Every quarter, the leadership team meets to decide: what's the breakthrough for this quarter? The founder is present, but speaks last (not first). The structure:

1. Each leader shares their perspective on the real constraint (written in advance, no founder input)
2. The team builds a shared diagnosis without founder input
3. The team debates which breakthrough would solve the constraint
4. The team decides (consensus or majority, but the team decides)
5. The founder speaks last: "Here's what I'm hearing. Here's my perspective. Here's the breakthrough I would pick." Often the founder agrees with the team. Sometimes the founder sees something the team missed. But the team's thinking comes first. The founder doesn't override.

Why it works: The team knows the founder will speak at the end. So they think deeply before that moment. They don't hold back ideas hoping the founder will validate them. They propose their best thinking. By the time the founder speaks, the room has done the intellectual work. The founder is adding perspective to thought, not providing thought to passive listeners.

Luke Bonney at Redox realized this dynamic. "It is impossible to be a participant in a discussion and a facilitator in a discussion." Once he stopped trying to do both, the leadership team's thinking shifted. The breakthroughs came from the team's reasoning, not from Luke's intuition. The result: "The really cool thing was the same narrative was what we were communicating internally and externally." One narrative, built by the whole team, not handed down by the founder.

**Structure 2: Anonymous Leadership Interviews**

Before the workshop, interview each leader individually and ask: "From the CEO perspective (not your functional perspective), what's genuinely strong about the company? What's genuinely broken? Where's the real bottleneck?"

The key: it's confidential. You never mention who said what. Not to the founder, not to the team.

When attribution disappears, people tell the truth. They name the bottleneck (often the founder). They surface grievances. They point out what's not working. None of this surfaces in the workshop (where attribution is clear and power dynamics are loud).

Compile the anonymous responses and share them with the leadership team (and the founder): "Here's what we're hearing." The conversation shifts from "what do I think?" to "what's actually true?" The team sees that others share their concerns. The founder sees what they can't see from inside the organization.

This creates the condition for real breakthrough diagnosis. You're not working from assumptions. You're working from data.

**Structure 3: Weekly Rocks Check-Ins (15 minutes)**

Every week, each department head shares: did we hit our rocks this week? Not: did we stay busy? Did we hit our targets?

This is 15 minutes, highly structured, fast. The founder is present but brief. The leader reports the outcome. If a rock is at risk, the team surfaces it and problem-solves right there.

The consistency creates a rhythm: every week, we measure whether we're moving the breakthrough forward. The team gets feedback constantly (weekly) instead of quarterly (at the next breakthrough workshop). The feedback loop is tight.

This also creates a condition: if a team member says "we didn't hit the rock," the founder's job is not to jump in and solve it. It's to ask: "What's blocking you?" Then listen. The team figures out the solution. The founder's role is clear: I'm here to see what's true, not to fix it.

### The Four Pitfalls to Avoid

**Pitfall 1: Fake Delegation.** The founder says "you decide," but the moment the team decides something the founder disagrees with, the founder overrides. The team learns: I don't actually have authority. I better figure out what the founder wants first. Windows of Wisdom disappear.

Prevention: If you're going to ask the team to decide, you have to execute their decision (or have a pre-agreed escalation: "I'll override if X happens," and you have to actually follow that threshold, not move the goalposts).

**Pitfall 2: Premature Delegation.** The team hasn't yet developed the thinking muscle, and the founder throws them into a decision without support. The team feels set up to fail. They lose confidence.

Prevention: Delegate on a spectrum. Week 1: I decide, team watches and asks questions. Week 2: Team decides with me in the room. Week 3: Team decides with me present but silent. Week 4: Team decides without me. By week 4, the team has context and skill. They're ready for real authority.

**Pitfall 3: Over-Correction.** The founder realizes they've been the bottleneck and swings too far: "I'm out of every decision. Figure it out." The team doesn't know what authority means. Everything stalls. The founder is now absent instead of present.

Prevention: Windows of Wisdom are specific and structured, not blanket absence. The quarterly workshop is structured (the founder speaks last, but they speak). The rocks check-in is structured (15 minutes, measure progress). The team knows when the founder is involved and when they're not.

**Pitfall 4: Leadership Team too Small.** If the leadership team is two people, anonymized interviews don't work. If the team is you + one person, there is no "team thinking" to unlock.

Prevention: Windows of Wisdom are most valuable with a leadership team of 4+ (enough people that thinking emerges from diversity of perspective). With a smaller team, the structures shift. Instead of "anonymous feedback from the team," it might be "direct conversation where I ask questions and listen." Instead of "the team decides," it might be "we decide together, and my job is to ask the question that makes your thinking better." Same principle (create conditions for thinking), different structure (suited to team size).

### The Timeline of Skill-Building

Week 1: Founder speaks first, team watches. Team is passive. Thinking is low.

Week 4: Team speaks, founder watches. Team is more active. Thinking is higher.

Week 8: Team decides with founder input. Team is thinking independently and integrating founder perspective. Thinking is high.

Week 12: Team decides, founder checks in after. Team is thinking without needing founder validation. Thinking is confident.

By month 6, the team's thinking muscle is rebuilt. They propose ideas confidently. They reason through problems without waiting. The founder is no longer the answer. The founder is the question-asker. The team is the answerer.
