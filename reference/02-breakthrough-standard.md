# THE BREAKTHROUGH STANDARD: FOUR PER YEAR, 25%+ EACH

*Background reading for the **Stock** phase of The Breakout Stack. Referenced by the skills that use these concepts -- not required reading up front.*

### What Is a Breakthrough?

Most companies measure progress by revenue growth. $5M this year, $6M next year. Growth, but no breakthroughs.

A breakthrough is different. It's a 25%+ move on a metric the board watches. That metric could be ARR, it could be customers acquired, it could be unit economics, it could be headcount. But it's specific, measurable, and meaningful — not an incremental improvement, but a structural shift.

The Startup Breakthrough Standard says this: Four breakthroughs per year (one per quarter) is the repeatable rhythm that produces exponential growth. Not four small improvements. Not four lucky months. Four deliberate, engineered, structural 25%+ moves.

### The Math

If you engineer a 25% improvement in a key metric every quarter, the compound effect is:

- Year 1: 1.25 × 1.25 × 1.25 × 1.25 = 2.44x
- Year 2: 2.44x × 2.44x = 5.96x
- Year 3: 5.96x × 2.44x = 14.5x

Fourteen-and-a-half times growth over three years. Not from each year compounding on the last. From the breakthroughs compounding. A company that goes from $2M to $29M in four years (Redox) is hitting this standard. Four breakthroughs per year, compounding for four years, produces that trajectory.

The math is mechanical. It's not inspirational. It's not motivational. It's just how compounding works.

### Why Four?

One breakthrough is luck. You hit a market shift, or a customer closes, or a partnership activates. That's not repeatable.

Two breakthroughs a year is intention. You pick a focus, you move the needle, it works. But one breakthrough every six months still feels like hoping.

Four breakthroughs a year (one per quarter) becomes a rhythm. A system. An expectation. Once you ship the breakthrough in Q1, the team knows: we do this again in Q2. And again in Q3. And again in Q4. The breakthrough becomes the operating system, not the anomaly.

By Q2, the team starts to understand what a breakthrough is and isn't. They stop proposing 3% improvements and start looking for 25%+ moves. The team's thinking shifts. They're not trying to be helpful (which produces incremental work). They're trying to move the constraint (which produces breakthroughs).

### The Difference Between Breakthrough and Progress

Progress is important. It's your day job. You're closing customers. You're shipping product. You're fixing bugs. All of that is necessary.

A breakthrough is different. It's a strategic move that changes the growth equation.

Shashank Bijapur at Spotdraft described his meta-breakthrough: "Reaching the point where product and GTM move together." That's not a process improvement. That's not a new hire. That's a structural shift in how the company operationally works. It's a 25%+ move in how fast the company can scale because the two functions that usually lag each other are now synchronized.

Kevin at Icetana identified his breakthrough differently: moving from "every deal is personal" to "we have a repeatable sales engine." Not hiring a VP of Sales (which would be an input). Moving the company from deal-based to systematic. That's a 25%+ shift in leverage.

The difference: progress is working harder. A breakthrough is changing the game.

### How Breakthroughs Get Blocked

The most common blocker: the founder is too busy to identify the breakthrough. Founder's calendar is full of firefighting, escalations, and execution. The strategic work (what is the breakthrough we should engineer this quarter?) gets pushed to the margins. The quarter ends. No breakthrough. Just progress.

Second blocker: the team doesn't have the authority to move on the breakthrough. The leadership team identifies what the breakthrough should be. The founder sees it differently. The quarter is spent debating. By Q3, the constraint has shifted and the original breakthrough is irrelevant.

Third blocker: the breakthrough gets diluted. The team commits to "we'll engineer a 25% move in customer acquisition." But the same quarter, the founder wants to fix the product experience, the board is pushing for a new market, and half the team is allocated to a customer crisis. The breakthrough gets 30% of attention instead of the 100% it needs. Nothing hits 25%.

The founder or the team or both need to be ruthless about protecting the breakthrough. One breakthrough per quarter means everything else that quarter is subordinate. Not suspended. Not ignored. But prioritized below the breakthrough.

### Building a Breakthrough

The standard structure is three phases:

**Phase 1: Diagnosis.** What's the real constraint right now? Not what the founder thinks. Not what the board is pushing for. What's the actual limiting factor? Icetana's constraint was sales (no owner, no repeatable process). Redox's was the leadership team's ability to operate as a network instead of a hub. Spotdraft's was product and GTM being misaligned. The diagnosis has to be right, because you're building the breakthrough around removing that constraint.

**Phase 2: Design.** What 25%+ move, if achieved, would solve the constraint? Not what would be nice. What would move the constraint? If the constraint is "we have a $500K sales bottleneck because every deal routes through the founder," the breakthrough might be "we move 80% of deals to non-founder closure within 90 days." That's 25%+ in sales leverage. That's specific. That's measurable.

**Phase 3: Execution.** The leadership team commits to the breakthrough. Not as an additional thing. As the thing everything else supports. The quarterly rocks (KPIs for each department) ladder up to the breakthrough. The weekly check-ins track progress toward the breakthrough. If something doesn't support the breakthrough, it's not on the Q1 roadmap — it's Q2 or Q3 work.

### What Happens Over Four Years

Companies that hit the Startup Breakthrough Standard don't look the same year to year.

A company that's at $2M and hits four breakthroughs a year is at roughly $5M by end of year 1. The team has seen what's possible. The org is small enough that the breakthroughs translate directly to results.

By year 2, the company is at $12M. The org has grown, which means coordinating the breakthrough is harder (more people to align, more functions to move together). But the team knows the rhythm. The culture is "we engineer growth, we don't hope for it."

By year 3, the company is at $29M. They've added 150+ people. The constraint has shifted multiple times — from sales, to operations, to product leadership, to fundraising. But each quarter, the leadership team names what the constraint is and engineers a breakthrough. The compounding from 12 quarters of breakthroughs is now visible.

By year 4, if the breakthroughs continued, the company would be at $71M. At scale, breakthroughs are harder to engineer (you can't just add headcount; the structure has to be right). Most companies stall here. But the ones that maintain the rhythm reach series C funding, operator-level exits, or sustained growth.

### The Founder's Role in Breakthroughs

The founder's job is not to engineer the breakthrough. It's to:

1. **Create the condition.** Time and focus for the leadership team to diagnose the real constraint. This means the founder isn't in every meeting, every decision, every approval. The founder clears space.

2. **Insist on one.** If the leadership team is trying to do five things, the founder's job is to say: "Pick the one breakthrough. What's the 25%+ move?" If the diagnosis is wrong, the founder's lived pattern-recognition can surface it. But the founder doesn't decide the breakthrough. The team does. The founder protects the process.

3. **Hold the line.** Protect the breakthrough from being diluted. When a customer crisis hits, when the board pushes for something different, when a team member wants to work on pet projects, the founder says: "Our Q1 breakthrough is X. That's where 80% of our energy goes. We'll handle the crisis, but it doesn't bump the breakthrough."

4. **Celebrate it.** The breakthrough is complete when the 25%+ move is achieved and sustained. The celebration isn't just "we hit our numbers." It's "we engineered a structural shift in how this company works." That belief in what's possible is what makes Q2 breakthroughs feel achievable instead of lucky.
